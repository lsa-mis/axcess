## Introduction: The STEAM Imperative in America

America is building an economy that runs on STEM skills while quietly dismantling the foundation that produces them. This is the defining contradiction of the next decade, and it is most acute in early childhood. The purpose of this white paper is to size that gap precisely and to show why the unmet need sits exactly where PARI STEAM World operates: the early years, before interest and confidence are lost.

### The economy is moving toward STEM. Fast.

STEM is no longer a specialized corner of the labor market; it is the labor market's growth engine. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics projects STEM occupations will grow 10.4% from 2023 to 2033, nearly triple the 3.6% projected for the rest of the economy (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2024). The wage premium is just as stark: STEM roles carried a median annual wage of $101,650 in May 2023, more than double the $46,680 median for non-STEM work (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2024). This is already a foundational pillar of the economy, not a niche — 36 million Americans, roughly a quarter of the workforce, were STEM workers in 2023 (NCSES/NSF, 2024).

The economic message to families could not be clearer, and parents have heard it: 76% of U.S. parents say they want their child to end up in a STEM-related career (The Toy Association, 2019).

### Yet the foundation is eroding.

Against that surging demand, achievement is moving the wrong direction. The 2024 Nation's Report Card recorded the lowest eighth-grade science performance in over a decade: only 31% of students reached NAEP Proficient, down four points from 2019, while 38% fell below NAEP Basic — the highest share since 2009 (NAGB/NCES, 2025). The decline compounds rather than corrects as students age. By twelfth grade, just 22% were proficient in math, with 45% below NAEP Basic, the largest share ever recorded (NAGB/NCES, 2025). Internationally, U.S. 15-year-olds scored 465 in math on PISA 2022 — below the OECD average, with 25 education systems outscoring them (NCES, 2023).

The root cause sits upstream, in the early grades this paper is concerned with. U.S. K–3 classrooms devote an average of just 18 minutes a day to science, against 89 minutes for English language arts and 57 for math (Horizon Research / 2018 NSSME, 2018). Children are, in effect, not exposed to science during the very window when scientific interest and identity form.

### The central paradox: curiosity without conversion.

The crisis is not a shortage of curiosity. Fully 75% of U.S. Gen Z report interest in STEM — yet only 29% name a STEM role as their first-choice career (Gallup and Walton Family Foundation, 2023). That interest-to-intent gap is the leak in the pipeline, and it is where economic potential drains away. The leak also has a clear equity dimension: 63% of girls express interest in STEM versus 85% of boys, a 22-point gap that appears before career choices are even made (Gallup and Walton Family Foundation, 2023).

So the paradox is fully formed: demand for STEM skills is accelerating and richly rewarded, while achievement falls, early-grade science is crowded out, and the abundant curiosity children start with converts poorly into commitment. The mismatch is structural, and it is widening.

### The demand is large — and the early years are where it concentrates.

This is not a problem in search of a market. The forces above sit on top of a large and fast-growing spend base. The U.S. educational toys market was an estimated $12.19 billion in 2023, growing at a 10.8% CAGR, and the standout signal is that STEM-specific demand within it is the fastest-growing sub-segment at a 15.0% CAGR — STEM is the growth engine, but still under-penetrated (Grand View Research, 2024). The digital layer is an order of magnitude larger: the U.S. EdTech market generated $47.74 billion in 2024 and is projected to reach $90.61 billion by 2030, and critically, **preschool is its fastest-growing sector** (Grand View Research, 2024). Households are already paying for enrichment outside school, through a U.S. online private tutoring market of $4.33 billion in 2024 (Grand View Research, 2024).

The pattern repeats across every verified layer: the fastest-growing slices are precisely the ones least served for early childhood.

### The thesis: a structural market gap between point solutions and what families need.

The conclusion this paper develops is that the failure is not one of money or of parental will — both are abundant — but of structure. Today's offerings are fragmented into mutually exclusive lanes: kit-only subscriptions, one-off STEM kits, and stand-alone apps. None integrates serialized story, a hands-on physical kit, and an adaptive companion into a single experience, and none centers diverse girl protagonists as role models. A parent who wants hands-on building, narrative motivation, and guided practice must today assemble several disconnected products and bills.

That is the white space, and it sits exactly where the stakes are highest: in the early-childhood window, where identity forms, where instruction is thinnest, and where high-quality intervention earns the highest documented returns. The sections that follow size that gap dimension by dimension — and make the case that closing it is both a commercial opportunity and a national imperative.
