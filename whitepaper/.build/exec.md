## Executive Summary

America's STEM future is being decided before children can read — and we are losing ground. The most recent Nation's Report Card shows just 31% of U.S. 8th graders reached proficiency in science in 2024, the worst result in over a decade, with 12th-grade math hitting a record low of 22% proficient (NAGB/NCES, 2025). The root cause sits upstream: K-3 classrooms devote a mere 18 minutes a day to science, against 89 for reading and 57 for math (Horizon Research, 2019). Children are not failing science — the system is failing to introduce it during the exact developmental window when curiosity and identity take shape.

That window is unforgiving, especially for girls. By age 6, girls become less likely than boys to associate brilliance with their own gender and begin avoiding activities for the "really, really smart" — a gap absent at age 5 (Bian, Leslie & Cimpian / *Science*, 2017). The damage compounds: the share of U.S. girls who believe they are good at math and science fell from 73% to 59% (ROX Girls' Index, 2024), and women remain just 26% of the STEM workforce (AAUW, 2023). Meanwhile, families are caught in a screen-time paradox — children ages 5-8 average 3.5 hours of daily screen media (Common Sense Media, 2025) — even as 78% of parents say they want more toys that build creativity and problem-solving (The Toy Association, 2025).

The market is large but fragmented. The U.S. educational toys market is projected to roughly double to about $24.6 billion by 2033 (Custom Market Insights, 2024), and U.S. EdTech will reach $90.6 billion by 2030, with preschool its fastest-growing segment (Grand View Research, 2024). Yet today's offerings are siloed: KiwiCo and Lovevery sell kits, Khan Academy Kids is an app, Osmo bolts pieces onto a tablet. None fuses serialized story, a hands-on kit, an AI companion, and diverse girl role models. That siloing drives churn — over one-third of subscribers cancel within three months (McKinsey, 2018) — because a disconnected craft-of-the-month gives kids no reason to return.

This is the white space PARI STEAM World occupies. The evidence is unusually aligned: hands-on active learning lifts performance by 0.47 standard deviations (Freeman et al. / PNAS, 2014); relatable STEM characters change real behavior, with heavy female *X-Files* viewers 50% more likely to have worked in STEM (Geena Davis Institute, 2018); and AI gains are real when it augments human guidance (Stanford Tutor CoPilot, 2024). PARI integrates all four proven levers — story, kit, supervised AI, and representation — into one engagement spine, intervening in early childhood before curiosity erodes and stereotypes harden. It is the only product purpose-built for the moment that matters most.
