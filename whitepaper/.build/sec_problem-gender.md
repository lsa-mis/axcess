## Problem 2 — The Representation Gap: Why Girls Opt Out

### A Pipeline That Leaks Before It Fills

Women are roughly half of the U.S. workforce, yet they remain a persistent minority in the fields that define the modern economy. Of the 36 million STEM workers in the United States in 2023, only 35% were women, and women hold just about 27% of core science and engineering occupations (NSF/NCSES, 2026). The shortfall is sharpest in precisely the disciplines PARI STEAM World centers. Women make up only about 16% of engineers and roughly 26% of computer and mathematical scientists (NSF/NCSES, 2023).

| STEM segment | Share of women | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Overall STEM workforce (2023) | 35% | NSF/NCSES, 2026 |
| Core science & engineering occupations | ~27% | NSF/NCSES, 2026 |
| Engineers | ~16% | NSF/NCSES, 2023 |
| Computer & mathematical scientists | ~26% | NSF/NCSES, 2023 |

Critically, this is not a question of ability or access to credentials. Women earn roughly half of all science and engineering bachelor's degrees, yet that near-parity collapses to about 24% of engineering and 22% of computing degrees (NSF/NCSES, 2024). A pipeline that begins at parity and ends at a quarter is leaking somewhere upstream — and the evidence points to early childhood.

### The Gap Opens at Age 6

The most rigorous developmental research locates the origin point with unusual precision. In a landmark study published in *Science*, by age 6 U.S. girls become less likely than boys to associate being "really, really smart" with their own gender, and begin actively avoiding activities described as being for "really, really smart" children — a gap that was not present at age 5 (Bian, Leslie & Cimpian, 2017). The "brilliance equals male" stereotype, in other words, is absent in kindergarten and measurable by first grade.

That early belief hardens with every additional year of schooling. A meta-analysis of 78 U.S. "Draw-A-Scientist" studies spanning five decades and more than 20,000 children found real cultural progress — the share drawing a female scientist rose from under 1% in 1966–1977 to 28% in 1985–2016 — yet the association of science with men still strengthens sharply with age. About 70% of 6-year-old girls draw a woman scientist, but only roughly 25% of 16-year-old girls do (Miller, Nolla, Eagly & Uttal, 2018).

| Age | Girls drawing a female scientist |
|---|---|
| 6 years | ~70% |
| 16 years | ~25% |

The attitudinal consequences are visible by adolescence. In a national survey of U.S. teens ages 13–17, only 9% of girls expressed interest in a STEM career, down from 11%, versus 27% for boys (Junior Achievement USA, 2019). Confidence erodes in parallel: the share of U.S. girls who believe they are good at math and science fell from 73% in 2017 to 59% in 2024 (ROX, 2024), with the steepest collapse in self-belief occurring in the early grades. By the time these girls reach eighth grade, the gap shows up in achievement itself — a statistically significant gender gap in NAEP science re-emerged in 2024, with boys outscoring girls after no measurable gap in 2019 (NAGB, 2025).

### Why Role Models Are the Decisive Lever

If the stereotype is learned, it can be unlearned — and the most rigorously evidenced countermeasure is exposure to relatable role models. The causal U.S. evidence is striking in its magnitude:

| Intervention | Measured effect | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Single guest talk by a woman economist | Women majoring in economics rose 8 points (9% → 17%) | Porter & Serra, via J-PAL, 2024 |
| Woman professor in intro math/science | Closed ~75% of the gender grade gap | Carrell, Page & West, via J-PAL, 2024 |
| Same-gender role-model exposure | Raised high-performing women's choice of underrepresented STEM majors by up to 26 points | Kofoed & McGovney, via J-PAL, 2024 |

Fictional role models work too, and their effects track real behavior, not just attitudes. In the Geena Davis Institute's "Scully Effect" study of roughly 2,000 U.S. women, 63% of those working in STEM named the fictional scientist Dana Scully a personal role model, and medium-to-heavy female viewers of *The X-Files* were 50% more likely to have actually worked in a STEM field than non- or light viewers (24% vs. 16%) (Geena Davis Institute, 2018). Demand for such representation is explicit: 71% of surveyed audiences say female STEM representation on-screen is important, up 20 points since 2018 (Geena Davis Institute, 2024).

### The Supply Deficit: Where Are the Girl Scientists?

Here the market fails the moment most decisively. Across 15 years of top U.S. television and film (2007–2022), women were just 37% of STEM characters in 2007–2017 and 38% in 2018–2022. The picture is bleaker still in the highest-leverage fields: female engineers were only 13% of on-screen STEM roles and computer scientists only 15% in 2018–2022 (Geena Davis Institute, 2024). The very stereotype that takes hold at age 6 is reinforced by media that overwhelmingly shows girls a male scientist.

The result is a precisely defined white space. The gender gap is seeded by a stereotype that emerges by age 6 and strengthens through adolescence; the proven antidote — relatable, same-gender role models — produces 8- to 26-point shifts in girls' STEM persistence; yet girls almost never see female STEM protagonists, even as more than seven in ten audiences say they want them. By placing two diverse girl protagonists, PARI and Sarala, at the center of serialized science adventures during the identity-forming early-childhood window, PARI STEAM World intervenes before the stereotype hardens — converting a documented, unmet, and explicitly demanded need into a defensible market position.
